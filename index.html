Den Den Town (でんでんタウン) — Full StrategyHOTEL DOORSTEP
Osaka's equivalent of Akihabara — and you're staying at it. The Hinode Hotel sits at the southern entrance of Den Den Town, with Ebisucho Station right next door. Two parallel streets cover everything: walk south down Sakaisuji for electronics, then back north up Ota Road (オタロード) for anime, retro games, trading cards, and figures. Over 150 shops in a compact 1km corridor. Don't stick to the main street — the best prices and rarest finds are in the back alleys. Half a day minimum. You have 5 evenings here. Use every single one.
Super Potato (スーパーポテト) — Ota Road: The anchor. The Osaka branch of Japan's most famous retro game store. Famicom, Super Famicom, Sega, Game Boy — every cartridge in great condition at prices that don't exist anywhere else. This is where vintage Game Boy DMG units and Pokémon Gold/Silver carts live. Browse the glass cases carefully. Start every Den Den Town session here. Trader 2 — Ota Road: Right alongside Super Potato. Good deals on retro hardware and software, slightly less picked-over than Super Potato. Check both on the same visit. Surugaya (駿河屋) — multiple branches: Two or three Surugaya branches in Den Den Town. Carries TCG singles including vintage Pokémon, figures, retro games, and manga. Prices are competitive. Good for Pokémon card hunting alongside Super Potato. Jungle — Ota Road: Specialist figure and collectibles store. Particularly good for unique finds and rarer items. Worth a sweep for anyone hunting specific Pokémon figures or limited edition goods. Mandarake (まんだらけ) — Ota Road: Multiple floors of secondhand manga, anime goods, TCG singles, and vintage figures. The Osaka branch is excellent for Pokémon card hunting. Less tourist traffic than the Akihabara branch so prices are often better. Animate Osaka Nipponbashi: The main anime merchandise chain. Good for current Pokémon TCG product, new figures, and licensed goods. Not vintage but comprehensive for current stock. Sofmap — Sakaisuji (north end): A huge multi-level secondhand electronics store at the northern edge of the strip. TVs, computers, gaming hardware, smartphones at rock-bottom prices. Good for a browse if you want to see what secondhand Japanese electronics look like. Practical notes: Shops open ~11am, close ~8pm. Small independents prefer cash — carry ¥10,000–20,000 (~CAD $95–190) on any serious hunting evening. Tax-free available at larger stores. Bigger shops accept cards; smaller ones often cash only. Evening browsing after dinner is a legitimate Den Den Town activity — the strip stays lively until closing.

KONBINI (CONVENIENCE STORES) ★
→7-Eleven, FamilyMart, and Lawson in Japan are extraordinary — nothing like back home. Japan's convenience store culture is a genuine part of daily life, not a fallback option. There is one within 50 metres of wherever you are at all times.
→Onigiri (おにぎり): The rice triangle is the cornerstone of konbini food culture. The wrapping is a small engineering marvel — pull the tabs in the right order and the nori stays crispy. Tuna mayo (avoid for Becca), salmon, kombu, mentaiko. ¥150-200 each (~CAD $1.50). Breakfast for the whole group costs about ¥1,000.
→Hot food counter: Fried chicken (karaage), steamed buns (nikuman), corn dogs, hot dogs. All kept warm behind the counter. Ask for the ones in the glass case — point and nod. Lawson's karaage is the most debated konbini food in Japan. FamilyMart's nikuman is exceptional in October when it's cold.
→Sandwiches: Japanese konbini sandwiches are a different category from Western sandwiches — egg salad, ham and cheese, katsu sando (pork cutlet). The egg salad sandwich has a genuine cult following. Light, sweet, nothing like what you'd expect. 🔴 Becca: check fillings — tuna mayo and egg mayo sandwiches are high-risk. Katsu sando and most hot sandwiches are safe.
→Sweets: Purin (Japanese custard pudding), cream puffs, matcha everything, seasonal items. The quality is genuinely excellent — not gas station food. Baked goods in the morning are often still warm.
→Coffee: The machine coffee at every konbini counter (100 yen, fresh ground) beats most Western café chains. BOSS canned coffee from the vending machine outside is a classic. Hot or cold.
→For Jem: Merries diapers (メリーズ) at virtually every konbini — the best diaper in the world. Kewpie baby food pouches, formula, wet wipes all stocked. A full Jem supply run takes 3 minutes.
→Services: ATM (7-Eleven accepts international cards reliably), Suica top-up, takuhaibin luggage forwarding, ticket printing, package pickup, photocopying, pay any bill.
→The honest truth: Some of the best meals of the trip will be eaten standing outside a konbini at 10pm. Embrace it completely.

🛁 ONSEN ETIQUETTE
→Shower first — always: Thoroughly wash your entire body at the shower station before entering the bath. This is non-negotiable. Shampoo, soap, rinse completely. The bath is for soaking, not cleaning.
→No towels in the water: Your small modesty towel stays outside the bath or folded on your head — never in the water. Most onsen provide two towels: a large one for drying after, a small one for modesty walking around.
→Enter slowly: The water is hot (40–43°C standard). Lower yourself in gradually. Your body adjusts within a minute or two. Don't splash or swim — still soaking only.
→Time limit: 15–20 minutes maximum per session, especially at natural hot spring temperature. Drink water before and after. Dizziness on standing up is common — stand slowly.
→Tattoos: Public onsen baths in Japan typically prohibit tattoos. The private family room (家族風呂 / Kazoku Buro) at Hinode Hotel has no such rule — you are the only guests in the room.
→What's provided: Hinode Hotel provides towels, soap, shampoo, and conditioner in the private room. No need to bring anything except yourselves and Jem.
→After the onsen: Pat dry gently — don't rub. The mineral water leaves a light coating on skin that moisturises naturally. Drink water. You will sleep extremely well.
→For Jem: See the dedicated onsen callout in the Jem tab — cool water to 37–38°C, maximum 10 minutes, rinse after. The private room makes all of this easy to manage.

🍚 JEM — FIRST SOLID FOODS IN JAPAN

🤱 FINDING NURSING ROOMS IN JAPAN (授乳室)
→The magic search term: 授乳室 (junyūshitsu). Type it into Google Maps anywhere in Japan and it shows every nursing room nearby with directions. This works in every city on the trip.
→Where to find them: Every department store (デパート), most train stations, shopping malls, and tourist attractions. Look for signs saying 授乳室 or 赤ちゃん休憩室 (baby rest room). They typically include a private nursing area, changing table, baby sink, and often a microwave for warming bottles.
→The phrase to say: 授乳室はありますか？ (Junyūshitsu wa arimasu ka?) — "Is there a nursing room?" Staff will lead you immediately and without hesitation. Japan is extraordinarily accommodating for nursing parents.

🇯🇵 JAPAN — SOCIETAL NORMS
→No eating or drinking while walking. Eating street food is done standing at the stall or sitting down nearby. Walking and eating — especially on busy streets — is considered rude. Konbini food is eaten outside the store or inside. Exception: ice cream cones are widely accepted.
→There are almost no public bins anywhere. This is the thing that catches every visitor off guard. You carry your rubbish until you find a konbini bin or get back to the hotel. Konbini bins outside stores are the main option. Carry a small bag in your daypack.
→Trains are quiet. No phone calls. Voices low. Headphones in. Set your phone to silent. Jem crying is universally understood and forgiven — don't stress about it. What people notice is adults being loud.
→Queue culture is absolute. Always queue in the marked lines. Never cut. At train platform doors, stand to the side of the yellow lines and let passengers off before boarding. This is observed almost universally.
→Escalators: stand on the left in Tokyo, stand on the right in Osaka. Walk on the other side. This flips between cities — just watch what everyone else is doing on arrival.
→Tipping is never done. Not at restaurants, taxis, hotels, or anywhere. Leaving money on the table can confuse or offend. The price on the bill is the price. No exceptions anywhere in Japan.
→Cash is still king at small shops. Many small restaurants, retro game shops, and independents in Den Den Town and Akihabara are cash only. Always carry ¥5,000–10,000 (~CAD $47–95).
→Chopstick etiquette. Never stick chopsticks upright in rice — it's a funeral association. Never pass food chopstick to chopstick — also a funeral ritual. Rest them on the holder or across the bowl.
→Shoes off. Any restaurant with tatami floor seating, traditional guesthouses, some temple interiors. Easy to spot — there will be a step up and a place to leave shoes. Just follow what others do.
→Public onsen rules (public only — not your private Hinode booking). Full nudity, no swimwear, no towels in the water. Wash thoroughly at the shower station before entering the bath. Tattoos can bar entry at some traditional establishments — less strictly enforced now but check. Your Hinode Hotel booking is a private family session — none of these rules apply to you.
→Bowing. A small nod or slight bow when someone helps you, serves you, or holds a door goes a long way. No need to go deep — just acknowledge. People genuinely appreciate it.
→Wet umbrella sleeves. Shops provide plastic sleeves for wet umbrellas at the entrance. Use them — don't drag a dripping umbrella through the store. The stand is right at the door.
→Rubbish sorting. Hotels and public spots have separate bins for burnable waste, plastic, cans, and PET bottles. Follow the labels. Getting it right is appreciated — getting it wrong is forgiven for tourists but noticed.
🇭🇰 HONG KONG — SOCIETAL NORMS
→Queue for everything. HK queues hard — MTR, taxis, escalators, dim sum restaurants. The queue is implicit even when not marked. Find the back of it and wait. Jumping it is noticed and frowned upon.
→Escalators: stand on the right, walk on the left. Opposite to Tokyo. The long Central–Mid-Levels escalator and every MTR escalator in HK follows this rule religiously.
→Cantonese first, always. Even a single word of Cantonese — màh-gōi (唔該), hóu sihk (好食) — opens doors that English won't. Older shopkeepers and market vendors respond visibly differently. Becca has a genuine superpower here.
→Bargaining is expected at markets, never at malls. Temple Street Night Market, Ladies Market — bargain freely, it's part of the experience. Starting at 50–60% of the asking price is normal. Malls, restaurants, and chain stores have fixed prices.
→Loud restaurants are normal. Dim sum restaurants and traditional HK eateries are genuinely noisy. Calling a waiter by raising your hand or saying màh-gōi loudly across the restaurant is standard and expected. Don't be shy.
→Priority seats on the MTR. Always give up priority seats for elderly, pregnant, or people with babies. Socially enforced very strictly in HK. With Jem you'll be the recipient — accept graciously with a nod.
→Eating while walking is more accepted than Japan but still considered casual or slightly rude in formal areas. Fine at markets and street stalls. Avoid on the MTR entirely — it's prohibited.
→Cash is common at markets, cards everywhere else. The MTR and most shops accept Visa/Mastercard contactless. Temple Street and Ladies Market: cash preferred for bargaining.
→The harbour view is sacred. Locals take genuine pride in the Victoria Harbour skyline — especially at night. Expressing genuine appreciation (wow, this is incredible) lands well with Popo and Goong Goong's connections and with anyone you meet.
